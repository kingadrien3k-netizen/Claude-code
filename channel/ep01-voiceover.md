## Part 2  (874 characters)

```
For about three weeks, perfect memory feels like the best thing that has ever happened to anyone. Here's the strange part: a few real people actually live with something close to this. It's called hyperthymesia, or highly superior autobiographical memory. People with it can recall nearly every day of their lives: what they did, what the weather was, what day of the week it fell on. And many of them describe it the same way: not as a gift, but as a movie that never stops playing. Because forgetting isn't your brain failing. It's your brain working. Every night, while you sleep, your brain weakens connections it doesn't need, a process linked to something called synaptic pruning. It clears space. It blurs the details so the important patterns can stand out. Forgetting is how you learn what matters. Wren's brain stopped doing that. By month two, the problems start.
```

## Part 3  (907 characters)

```
Every argument Wren has ever had replays with perfect clarity. Every embarrassing moment. Every word someone said and maybe didn't mean. Wren can't "get over" anything, because nothing ever gets old. A mistake from six weeks ago feels exactly like a mistake from six seconds ago. Simple things get hard. A crowded room means a thousand new memories every minute, and none of them ever leave. Wren starts staying home. Closing the curtains. Sitting in the dark, because darkness gives the memory nothing new to save. Wren starts to understand something: a perfect memory doesn't just keep the good moments. It keeps everything, and it never lets you choose. Then, on a Thursday in the fourth month, Wren is sitting in the kitchen, sorting through memories like old photos… and finds one that doesn't fit. It's a memory of a train station. A red umbrella. A woman saying, "You're late, but I knew you'd come."
```

## Part 4  (683 characters)

A hospital waiting room. Memories with dates on them… dates that haven't come. Wren's brain hadn't just stopped forgetting. It had stopped caring which direction time was going. Three weeks later, Wren stands on that platform, holding a red umbrella. The woman turns around. "You're late," she says, smiling. "But I knew you'd come." And Wren, who can now remember everything that ever was, and everything that will be, realizes the one thing that perfect memory can never give back. Surprise.
